What Should a Temporal Contact Generator Actually Model?

From dense hand–object contact trajectories to learned structural dynamics.

The argument in 30 seconds

  1. MotivationHow do we generate better hand motion?

    Contact prediction is a major source of hand-motion error. In the two-stage generator we studied (object motion → contact → hand motion), replacing the recorded contact with the predicted one raises the hand error on all four TACO test splits and in all eight ARCTIC windows we checked.

  2. Finding 1What varies?

    A contact trajectory is an initial grasp mode plus a temporal evolution. The evolution itself mixes structural change with reconfigurations that leave the grasp’s modelled wrench capability nearly unchanged.

  3. Finding 2What matters?

    A compact structural state is enough for the modelled wrench capability, and predicts future structured contact at least as well as the full dense map. Which parts touch, how much, where, and facing which way. The remaining dense detail is real and persistent, but its change is weakly determined.

  4. Finding 3How should it be modelled?

    The structural state can be learned as a latent z; its dynamics remain the likely bottleneck. Supervising the handcrafted structure did not help in the one form we tried. A latent z taught by distances between those structure descriptors concentrates the structure and is temporally meaningful. What most likely limits it is predicting its whole trajectory for sequences it was not trained on.

  5. Current hypothesisnot a finding

    Carry z statefully through time, and keep the known first contact map s0 when decoding. A 2 × 2 experiment is running; no result is reported here.

Motivation

How do we generate better hand motion?

In the two-stage generator we studied, contact prediction is a major source of the downstream hand error.

One way to generate two-handed manipulation for a given object motion is to go through contact: first predict where the hands touch the object over time, then generate hand motion that realizes those contacts. BimArt (CVPR 2025) is a diffusion model of this kind, with a contact stage and a motion stage.

Evidence · same motion model, two contact inputs

With predicted contact, the hand error is higher on every split

In our port of BimArt to TACO, the same motion model, started from the same initial noise, was conditioned once on the recorded contact map and once on the contact stage’s prediction. The mean hand-keypoint error rises on all four test splits: 37.1 → 58.6, 48.8 → 86.5, 63.4 → 104.9 and 50.9 → 79.8 mm.

The released BimArt model shows the same direction on its own data: on eight ARCTIC laptop test windows the error is higher with the predicted contact in every window.

test_1 was the validation split used to select the checkpoints; test_2 to test_4 are the unbiased splits, and the error rises on all of them. Read as “a major source of error in this pipeline”, not “the only one” and not a statement about every generator: the evidence is for one two-stage model family, and with the recorded contact the motion stage is still 37–63 mm off on TACO. The recorded-contact arm is an oracle. The TACO numbers come from this project’s own port (one training run, 128 windows per split, no confidence interval). The ARCTIC numbers are eight windows from two sequences of one object, five of them from one sequence, so the size of that gap should not be read as typical. We do not quote a percentage share of the error due to contact: the stage contributions do not add linearly.

What should a temporal contact generator actually model?

Finding 1 · What varies?

A contact trajectory is an initial grasp mode plus a structured temporal evolution

A large part of the multimodality is associated with which first contact map a sequence starts from. What follows is partly structural change and partly reconfiguration that leaves the grasp’s modelled function nearly unchanged.

Throughout, a contact map Ct is a soft contact value on 512 canonical points of the object surface, per hand and per frame. G is the object geometry, τ the object motion, and s0 the first contact map of a 64-frame sequence that starts at the onset of a contact episode (its first frame of firm contact; on ARCTIC about half of the test sequences start at a later episode of the same take). These studies use no grasp labels: “grasp mode” here means which s0 a sequence starts from.

Main evidence A · within-take and between-take variation

On TACO the amount of contact changes more within a take than between takes; the spatial layout changes less within a take than across meshes (TACO) or object instances (OakInk2), and only slightly less than across subjects on ARCTIC

On TACO, early and late frames of one take differ in contact amount about twice as much as two different takes do at the same phase (ratio 2.12). Their normalized spatial pattern differs about as much as between two takes (1.04) and less than between two meshes (mesh / take 1.48; time / mesh 0.69).

On ARCTIC and OakInk2 the within-take change of the pattern is at or below the take-to-take level (0.94 and 0.45). On OakInk2, frames from a different object instance differ in pattern 1.69 times as much as frames from a different take (over the 17 of 39 groups in which two instances share a verb). ARCTIC has one mesh per object, so its identity axis is the subject: frames from another subject differ in pattern 1.16 times as much as frames from another take of the same subject (1.01 against the take level used for the time ratio). The temporal excess of the amount differs by dataset, in line with what a take contains: 2.12 on TACO takes that include approach and release, 1.10 on ARCTIC’s long multi-episode takes, 0.60 on OakInk2’s interaction-only segments.

This does not say that contact does not move over time: the spatial pattern changes with the time lag on all three datasets. The statement is relative. Within a take, the temporal change of the pattern is smaller than the change associated with mesh (TACO, 0.69 of it) or object instance (OakInk2, 0.36), and slightly smaller than the change associated with subject on ARCTIC (0.93; 6 of 22 groups are above 1). On TACO a mesh is confounded with the people who used it.

Main evidence B · sample the first contact, then evolve it

Much of the multimodality is associated with the choice of the initial contact map

p(C1:T | G, τ)≈p(s0 | G)·p(C1:T | s0, G, τ)

A diffusion sampler draws s0 from the static object condition (its geometry and which hand); a deterministic vector field then evolves it with the object motion. On TACO, holding the true s0 for 64 frames gives a contact error of 3.48; evolving it gives 1.82. With a sampled s0 the error is 2.59 for one sample and 1.86 for the best of ten (picked with the ground truth), whose paired difference from the rollout started at the true s0 has an interval that contains zero (+0.04, 95% interval −0.04 to +0.12).

ARCTIC and OakInk2 do not show the same benefit from the evolution: the paired difference between evolving and holding the true s0 has an interval that contains zero (−0.07 [−0.26, +0.11] and −0.02 [−0.11, +0.06]), and the best of ten stays above the true-s0 rollout (+0.34 and +1.27). There the evolution from the true s0 makes no measurable difference, and the large differences between the variants come from the initial map: one sampled s0 adds +1.56 on ARCTIC and +2.85 on OakInk2 to the error of the true-s0 rollout (2.14 and 1.24), so the initial map is the larger part of the error on OakInk2 but not on ARCTIC.

How to read this

The interpretation the sources support is that a large part of the multimodality is associated with the choice of the initial contact map. They do not show that the future is deterministic once s0 is fixed; that question is tested in Finding 3, step A.

One training seed and one fixed split per dataset. From a single sampled map on ARCTIC, evolving is significantly worse than holding that map (+0.25 [+0.07, +0.44]).

Main evidence C · where the temporal model does and does not help

Contact change is concentrated in a minority of transitions, and on the spatial ones the evolved model is no better than holding the map still

Transitions whose frame-to-frame change exceeds the training 90th percentile are 12.6% of the TACO test transitions, 5.7% on ARCTIC and 9.3% on OakInk2. They hold 70%, 62% and 72% of the squared change. On these large changes the model evolved from the true first map stays close to the static baseline. For spatial-dominant changes its temporal error (the error of the predicted frame-to-frame change; for holding the map still this equals the size of the true change) is essentially unchanged on all three datasets (0.97 → 0.96 on TACO, 0.81 → 0.82 on ARCTIC, 0.485 → 0.484 on OakInk2). Its clearest reduction is on amount-dominant changes on TACO (0.88 → 0.69); mixed changes on TACO sit in between (0.99 → 0.92).

Temporal error by kind of transition, for holding the first map and for the evolved model. On TACO the model’s large gain in absolute error is about the same on non-spike and on spike frames (3.42 → 1.77 and 3.79 → 2.10). The report attributes it to the slow trend of the contact amount accumulated over the window, not to following the large changes themselves, whose temporal error barely moves (0.98 → 0.92 on all spike frames).

This is a statement per transition, not about the total: most of the model’s summed temporal error lies on non-spike frames (64%, 77% and 66%). No paired test between the two models exists in this analysis, and on TACO and OakInk2 many spatial-dominant and mixed spikes are short-lived (the change cancels within a few frames).

Supporting evidence: does the hand tell us when contact will change? association is not prediction

Sub-evidence A

Hand motion relative to the object is associated with contact change

At the same transition, the size of the contact change correlates with the hand’s speed relative to the object: Spearman 0.78 on TACO and 0.51 on ARCTIC. Object motion alone correlates far less.

The TACO value uses a corrected tool-frame transform; the original event analysis reported 0.68 for the same quantity (the reference line). On TACO, object speed is the target object’s world speed, also in sequences where the hand holds the tool. This is an association at the same frame, not a prediction result.

TACOARCTICSource: Temporal contact events · Hand-information diagnostic

Sub-evidence B

That association gives little prediction of the future contact from past hand frames

Added to a predictor that already sees the current contact, the object and its motion (from eight frames back to eight frames ahead, since the object trajectory is a given input), the current hand pose does not lower the error of the contact change four frames ahead. Hand frames up to the current one (eight frames of history) give a small gain (−2.4% on TACO, −2.1% on ARCTIC); hand motion without absolute pose gives more (−6.8%, −4.2%). An oracle that is also given the next eight hand frames gains far more (−17.7%, −15.6%).

Hand motion is not irrelevant: it is associated with contact change. The point is the difference between simultaneity and predictive information, and it is a matter of degree. Against a shuffled-hand control of the same size, the gain of the hand history holds on TACO and is not distinguishable from zero on ARCTIC, where the no-hand predictor is itself worse than predicting no change in mean error. Hands are ground truth; three seeds.

TACOARCTICSource: Hand-information diagnostic

Past hand frames add little to predicting when a persistent contact transition starts

For “a persistent contact transition starts within four frames”, hand inputs up to the current frame add little: on TACO the area under the precision–recall curve goes from 0.33 without the hand (chance is 0.10) to 0.36 with eight frames of history, a paired gain of +0.03 that the current pose alone already gives; on ARCTIC there is no gain over 0.13. The future-hand oracle reaches 0.43 and 0.28. For the size of the contact change, the gain from past hand frames is largest on release, then onset and amount-dominant frames; where contact re-arranges at roughly constant amount (persistent spatial frames) it is small on TACO and absent on ARCTIC.

The exception

Events that change the amount, or the set of touching parts, change capability more often

46% of persistent mixed events gain more than 25% capability on both datasets, against 20% (TACO) and 23% (ARCTIC) of persistent spatial ones; about half of the mixed events still stay within ±25%. Finding 1 · conclusion

Temporal contact evolution contains both meaningful structural evolution and function-preserving realization variation.

Much of the multimodality is associated with the first contact map (clearly on TACO; on ARCTIC and OakInk2 the evolution shows no measurable benefit over holding the true first map). What follows is partly a change of how much and which parts touch, and partly a re-arrangement that, in the typical event on TACO and ARCTIC (the wrench analysis does not cover OakInk2), leaves the modelled capability almost unchanged.

Which part of the dense contact state actually matters?

Finding 2 · What matters?

Dense contact contains a compact structural state

On the tested ladder, forty-eight numbers per frame (which hand parts touch, how much, where, and facing which way) are the smallest level that keeps both the modelled wrench capability and the prediction of future structured contact at least as well as the full dense state does under fixed small models. The dense detail left over persists, but its change is hard to predict.

R2 is therefore not only a wrench descriptor: adding centroid and normal also lowers the future-structure error relative to R1, by 12% (TACO) and 15% (ARCTIC) as a mean over the four targets. The gain comes from the future centroid / normal and the future wrench profile; the future amount (both datasets) and participation (ARCTIC) are not predicted better. By the study’s saturation rule, which compares each level with the full dense state, the same 48 numbers are the smallest level saturated on both questions on both datasets at the 2.5% and 5% tolerances (at 10%, R1 already passes on TACO; on the temporal question alone R1 passes on both datasets, so it is the wrench question that selects R2). The rule is passed because the dense state is worse under the fixed models, so R2 is close to the best level rather than the best: its wrench error is 13% (TACO) and 8% (ARCTIC) above the best level, its future-structure error 4% and 2% above.

These are comparisons between inputs under one fixed probe and one fixed training recipe, not statements about information content. The report treats R2 as a function of the dense hand–contact state (contact map plus hand) and reads the dense inputs being worse as a statement about the parameterization (the 512-point map carries no finger labels and its point positions differ from object to object) and about the fixed small models; the dense inputs remain better at predicting the dense future itself. Nor is this good absolute prediction: by the per-frame errors, one frame ahead the R2 model does not beat carrying the current value forward on either dataset. Eight frames ahead it does on TACO for participation, amount, normal and wrench profile (not for the centroid); on ARCTIC it does not for amount, centroid, normal or wrench profile, and for participation the two scores disagree. The temporal models were early-stopped.

Main evidence C · what R2 leaves out

The remaining dense residual behaves differently

How much of the dense state R2 determines

A decoder from R2 alone reproduces 89% of the variance of the contact map and 85% of the hand points on TACO, and 74% and 69% on ARCTIC. The residual is 11–15% of the dense variance on TACO and 26–31% on ARCTIC.

The residual persists, but its change is weakly predictable

Carrying the current contact-map residual forward leaves only 19% (TACO) and 9% (ARCTIC) of its energy unexplained one frame ahead, rising to 75% and 42% at eight frames. Against that persistence baseline (the line at 1), a causal predictor that sees the dense history, the residual’s own past and the object’s trajectory removes 4%, 14% and 20% of the error at 1, 4 and 8 frames on TACO and nothing measurable on ARCTIC. An oracle that is also given the future structure does no better.

The residual is what this particular decoder leaves, not a canonical decomposition, and it is real dense detail, not noise. The hand-point residual (not drawn) is an exception: on TACO its change is partly predicted even causally (22–46% of the last-value error, 31–62% with the future structure), and at eight frames its last value is no better than a zero residual; on ARCTIC the causal gain is 11% one frame ahead and not distinguishable from zero later (19–24% with the future structure). “Not predictable” means not by these models from this context.

Supporting evidence: would a richer description help? coarse hand pose, contact topology

Sub-evidence A

Coarse hand pose adds little

Adding a coarse hand pose to R2 leaves the wrench error unchanged on both datasets (ratio 1.00 and 1.01). The future-structure error falls by about 2% on TACO, averaged over horizons (it rises one frame ahead and falls by 4–5% at four and eight frames), and not at all on ARCTIC. The coarse hand does help predict the future hand itself and the dense contact map.

Finding 2 · conclusion

Under the tested probes, the full dense contact map gave no advantage over a compact structural state for the wrench capability or for predicting future structured contact. The compact state captures most of that information, while the remaining dense realization is persistent but weakly determined.

The share left to the realization differs by dataset: 11–15% of the dense variance on TACO, 26–31% on ARCTIC.

Can this boundary be learned instead of manually encoded?

Finding 3 · How should we model what matters?

The structural state can be learned as a latent z, but its dynamics remain the likely bottleneck

Predicting the handcrafted structure explicitly does not help. A learned latent concentrates the structure and moves in step with the contact. What does not yet work is predicting its whole trajectory for a new sequence.

The task is fixed in this section: given the first contact map s0, the object geometry G and the object motion τ, generate the next 63 contact maps of a 64-frame sequence. These experiments were run on TACO and ARCTIC, each read separately; OakInk2 was not part of them. Generation errors in this section are averages over the 63 generated frames, so holding the first map still scores 3.54 on TACO and 2.24 on ARCTIC here, against 3.48 and 2.21 in Finding 1, where the given first frame is counted too.

  1. Step A · explicit structural supervision

    Explicit structural supervision does not help one surrogate tested

    The direct way to use Finding 2 is to add its structure as a training signal to a dense generator: a loss on the structure extracted from the generated map, and on top of that a hidden head that predicts it. Neither improves the deterministic generator. With the true s0 given, the output loss makes the participation, centroid, normal and wrench-profile errors 1.8–5.4% higher on TACO; on ARCTIC centroid, normal and wrench are 3.1–4.9% higher and participation is not distinguishable from no change (+0.8%, interval includes zero). Only the contact-amount error is lower, by under 1%, and on ARCTIC even that is not distinguishable from no change (interval includes zero). The hidden head does not rescue it.

    Structure loss: no gain

    Relative change of each test error when the supervision is added; above zero is worse. Bars: 95% take-bootstrap intervals.

    A stochastic future adds dense jitter

    With s0 fixed, one sample of the sequence-diffusion model trained with the dense loss only has no better typical structure than the matching deterministic model, a higher dense error (+15% TACO, +27% ARCTIC), and maps that change far more from frame to frame than real ones (1.15 against 0.31 on TACO; 1.59 against 0.16 on ARCTIC).

    Sampling the first map gives more structural spread than sampling the future

    Ten sampled s0 evolved deterministically differ more in their structure than ten diffusion futures from one fixed s0: 1.63 against 0.82 on TACO, 2.48 against 1.15 on ARCTIC. The wrench profiles show the same order (0.27 against 0.11; 0.55 against 0.18).

    Three training seeds. One structural surrogate and one diffusion recipe were tried. The structure loss and the structural test metrics both use the true hand-part label of each surface point. The initial sampler is the frozen sampler of Finding 1. The two diversity measurements use different protocols (true versus sampled s0), and diversity measures spread, not coverage of real alternatives: only one human future is observed per condition. The supported reading is narrow: the structure, useful as an analysis representation, is not necessarily useful as an explicit output target; and the results are consistent with placing the stochasticity at the initial grasp selection rather than in a diffusion of the future after s0 is fixed.

  2. Step B · learn the representation instead

    A learned z / r factorization concentrates the structure in z partial

    Stage 1 is a single-frame autoencoder. One encoder maps the contact map to a 64-D latent z, and a decoder turns z back into a “z-only” map. A second encoder and decoder add a 64-D code r that only corrects what the z-only map misses. z is shaped by a relational loss: distances between the z of different frames are matched to distances between their structure and wrench descriptors from Finding 2. No output is ever compared with the handcrafted structure itself.

    z keeps the probed structure; r keeps little of it

    A small probe reading z recovers the structure descriptor and the wrench profile about as well as the same probe reading the 512-D map (a mean over five quantities of 1.07 on TACO and 1.10 on ARCTIC; dense map = 1). Reading r, it recovers little on average: 0.03 on TACO, and −0.53 on ARCTIC, which is worse than predicting the training mean.

    r completes the dense reconstruction

    Adding r lowers the reconstruction error on the unique test frames from 0.449 to 0.151 on TACO and from 0.645 to 0.205 on ARCTIC. The first two bars (plain autoencoder, PCA-128) are 128-D references; z alone has 64 dimensions, and the 64-D linear reference (PCA-64, not drawn) is at 0.66 and 1.19. r carries complementary dense detail that z does not reconstruct. That detail is a small part of the map: z alone already explains 98.4% and 95.7% of its variance.

    In latent swaps, structure follows z

    Decoding the z of one frame with the r of another: the structure follows the z donor in 97% (TACO) and 98% (ARCTIC) of swaps. The dense detail follows the r donor in 77% on TACO but only 44% on ARCTIC.

    This is a preferential factorization, not disentanglement: z and r are not independent (the r branch reads z), and the split is taught by the relational teacher. Without that loss the same architecture gives a z that keeps less structure (probe score 0.77 on TACO and 0.63 on ARCTIC). The probes measure the same quantities the teacher was built from, on held-out takes, and their scores have no interval. The structure half of the swap is largely built into the architecture (a swapped map is the host’s z-only map plus a small correction); the detail half is the informative one. r is about as persistent as the dense residual it encodes (autocorrelation at 8 frames 0.65 against 0.69 on TACO, 0.80 against 0.77 on ARCTIC), so it is not a fixed sequence-level code. One training seed. By the study’s own rule the outcome is a partial factorization on both datasets.

    Local z dynamics exist, especially on TACO

    Given the true current z and the object motion around the two frames, how much does a learned single-step predictor improve on simply copying z? In a split added after the results were seen, the gain is clear on both datasets for pairs that start at the first frame of a sequence; for pairs that start eight or more frames in, it is smaller on TACO (2%, 9% and 14%), and on ARCTIC the predictor is worse than persistence one frame ahead (−9%) and indistinguishable from it four frames ahead.

Supporting evidence: is it the decoder, or the latent trajectory? decoder adaptation, train–test gap

The decoder changes we tried do not solve it; the predicted latent trajectory has a large train–test gap.

Sub-evidence A

Decoder adaptation does not solve the problem

A larger decoder, a longer training schedule and checkpoint selection on the dense loss, changed together, leave the dense error “similar” by the study’s rule: 1.698 against 1.732 on TACO (a 1.96% reduction whose interval excludes zero, but under the study’s 2% threshold and absent at a matched training step) and 2.158 against 2.157 on ARCTIC. Fed the same predicted latent, the old and the new decoder are within 0.5% of each other; fed the true latent, both decode at about 0.5 (TACO) and 0.7 (ARCTIC).

The earlier model had already been trained jointly on predicted latents, so this is not “frozen versus adapted”. A wider decoder and a decoder trained on many held-out predictions were not tested; the report’s own answer on decoder mismatch is “no evidence was found that this mismatch caused the failure”.

TACOARCTICSource: z joint-decoder follow-up

Sub-evidence B

The latent trajectory is fitted on training sequences, not on new ones

With the same weights of the adapted model, the predicted z is close to the true one on training sequences (error 0.09 on TACO, 0.06 on ARCTIC; the earlier model: 0.22 and 0.19) and far from it on held-out ones (validation / test 0.63 / 0.70 and 0.80 / 0.72). On a subset of 160 training sequences the adapted model’s predicted latent decodes at about the same error as the true one; on test sequences it decodes at 1.70 against 0.51 (TACO) and 2.16 against 0.69 (ARCTIC).

The held-out prediction is not uninformative: it stays below the error of predicting the training mean (0.99 and 0.91). The decoder fed the true latent is itself about three times worse on test than on those training sequences, so not all of the train–test difference of the decoded error is due to the predicted latent. The report’s own wording for “the latent trajectory is the bottleneck” is “most likely”.

Finding 3 · conclusion

A learned structural state z exists and is temporally meaningful. The evidence does not point to the existence of z, or to the decoders we tried, as what is missing. It points to how the latent trajectory is evolved and generalized from the initial observation.

The datasets differ and the difference is part of the result. On TACO the z-mediated generator ties the direct one on dense error and improves most structural errors, and local z dynamics beat persistence. On ARCTIC the z-mediated generator is worse on dense error and z is persistence-dominated. The test sets differ as well: every group, mesh and subject of the TACO test set is also seen in training, while 21 of the 44 ARCTIC test takes come from a subject absent from training (the diagnostic’s ARCTIC reading is the same on the takes of seen subjects).

How should the learned structural state evolve?

Two changes are being tested: carry z forward as a state, and keep the known first contact map when decoding. Neither has a result yet.

Everything above is a completed study. This section is not. It states the two hypotheses the completed evidence motivates and the experiment that is running to test them.

Hypothesis A

Stateful structural evolution

Today all 63 future latents are predicted in one pass from (s0, G, τ); no latent state is carried forward. The candidate predicts an initial state from (s0, G) with its own initial-state module (the Stage-1 encoder’s architecture, trained from scratch inside the model with none of its weights) and then advances it, zt+1 = zt + F(zt, G, τ), where τ is the object motion around frame t. It is trained and run as a rollout on its own predictions, without teacher forcing.

Why it is plausible: given the true current latent, a learned predictor beats persistence on TACO (on ARCTIC only marginally, and only eight frames ahead), and the true latent decodes well.

The diagnostic that motivates this measured single steps from the true latent, not a rollout; a rollout feeds its own errors back and sees no observation while it runs. Started from the true first latent, the single-step predictor’s advantage over the whole-sequence model shrinks quickly with the target frame (51%, 17% and 5% at frames 1, 4 and 8 on TACO; 42%, 10% and 1% on ARCTIC). The joint-decoder follow-up rated the stateful test “a reasonable next test on TACO and a weak one on ARCTIC”.

Hypothesis B

Preserve the initial dense realization

Today the decoder rebuilds every map from the latent and the geometry, Ct = D(zt, G), without access to the given s0. The candidate keeps the known first map and decodes only its change: Ct = s0 + DΔ(s0, zt, G).

Why it is plausible: the Stage-2 z-mediated model is already behind at the first generated frame (0.94 against 0.36 on TACO, 1.27 against 0.40 on ARCTIC), and the direct model already predicts a change from s0.

s0 preserves the initial realization while z controls the structural evolution: that is the intended reading, not a measured one. In the current model only the decoder lacks s0; the temporal network is conditioned on it.

How to read this page

  • Every number is read from the project’s result tables. Charts are drawn from those tables by docs/build; each chart has a “Data table” with its values, a note on how to read them, and its source files.
  • evidence_manifest.json maps the claims of every evidence block to their report, result files, metric and numbers. content_outline.md lists the evidence under each finding.
  • TACO and ARCTIC results are never pooled. Raw contact-error units differ between datasets: compare within a dataset.
  • From evidence 1B on, the TACO test set is the dataset’s test_1 split (364 sequences from 186 takes; every group, mesh and subject also appears in training). The ARCTIC test set is 186 sequences from 44 takes, 21 of them from a subject absent from training.
  • Intervals are bootstrap intervals over takes. Most model experiments use one training seed (the structure / variance probes, the structure-aware generation and the hand-information studies use three), so the intervals describe the test set, not training randomness.
  • Wrench capability is a modelled proxy for functional grasp capability, under a simplified friction-cone model.
