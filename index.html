Understanding Structure in Human–Object Contact Dynamics

Studying how dense human–object contact trajectories decompose into grasp-level structure, temporal evolution, and realization variation.

Observations from ongoing work, not a finished method or published result.

In one minute

  1. Choosing a grasp realization and evolving it through time are different sources of contact variation: not the same problem.
  2. Not every dense contact reconfiguration meaningfully changes the modelled wrench capability.
  3. A compact structural state keeps much of what matters for modelled wrench capability and future structured contact.
  4. We are investigating whether contact generation should be modelled as structured contact dynamics, not unrestricted dense trajectory generation.

Motivation

Why contact?

One way to generate hand motion goes through contact: object trajectory → contact → hand motion.

TACO

In the two-stage generator we studied (BimArt, CVPR 2025; our port to TACO), replacing the recorded contact with the contact stage’s prediction raises the mean hand error on all four TACO test splits: 37.1 → 58.6, 48.8 → 86.5, 63.4 → 104.9 and 50.9 → 79.8 mm.

TACO Source: two-stage generator study

These results suggest that contact-stage errors can substantially affect downstream hand generation.

Technical note

One model family; one training run of our TACO port; split 1 also served for model selection. The recorded-contact input is an oracle, measured from the recorded hands; with it the hand is still 37 to 63 mm off, so contact is a major source of error, not the only one. We quote no percentage share: the two stages’ contributions do not add. No confidence interval exists for the gap.

Observation 1

What varies across a human contact trajectory?

Choosing a grasp realization and evolving it through time are different sources of variation.

Without grasp labels, “grasp realization” is proxied by take, object or subject identity and the first contact map.

TACO ARCTIC OakInk2 Source: dynamic-contact analysis

Within a take (one recording), the spatial pattern changes about as much as between two takes on TACO, slightly less on ARCTIC and clearly less on OakInk2, and less than across object meshes or instances. The contact amount differs about twice as much within a TACO take, which includes approach and release, as between takes; not within ARCTIC’s long multi-episode takes (1.10) or OakInk2’s interaction-only segments (0.60). The pattern does change over time: the statement is relative.

This motivates a hierarchical view

p(C1:T | G, τ) ≈ p(s0 | G) · p(C1:T | s0, G, τ)

Choose an initial contact realization (first contact map s0), then model its evolution (C: contact maps; G: object geometry; τ: object motion). On TACO, a learned model that evolves the true first map clearly beats holding it (error 3.48 → 1.82); on ARCTIC and OakInk2 the difference is not distinguishable from zero, although contact still changes there.

TACO ARCTIC OakInk2 Source: hold-versus-evolve comparison
Technical note

Ratios are means over groups; intervals exist per group only. On TACO an object mesh is confounded with the people who used it. ARCTIC’s third bar is relative to two takes of the same subject (1.01 against the level of its other bars); OakInk2’s covers fewer than half of its groups. Holding and evolving both start from the true first map: diagnostic conditions, not generators. Held → evolved: ARCTIC 2.21 → 2.14 (paired difference −0.07, 95% interval −0.26 to +0.11); OakInk2 1.26 → 1.24 (−0.02, −0.11 to +0.06). One model family, one training run; error units differ between datasets.

Interpretation

Which contact a sequence starts from and how it evolves are different problems; how much the second adds differs with the interaction regime.

The evolution itself is not all of one kind.

Observation 2

Does every visible contact reconfiguration change what the grasp can do?

Large contact change does not necessarily mean functional change.

A friction-cone grasp model gives the force and torque a grasp can apply along 76 directions: its modelled wrench capability, a proxy for functional grasp capability. Retention: the share of the later grasp’s capability that the earlier one already had.

TACO ARCTIC Source: wrench-capability analysis

Within-grasp spatial reconfiguration keeps most modelled capability

TACO 0.85 119 events

ARCTIC 0.89 26 events; 95% interval 0.76 to 0.96

TACO 0.97

ARCTIC 0.96

On TACO contact-change size says little about capability change (rank correlation 0.19); on ARCTIC (0.55) 5 of the 11 larger changes gain more than 25%.

TACO ARCTIC

Counterpoint: other changes alter it more often

TACO ARCTIC
Technical note

Capability comes from a simplified single-hand grasp model: one unit of force per touching hand part, linearised friction, no object mass or force limits; values are relative, not physical forces. Events: persistent spatial reconfigurations in a maintained grasp; medians over small classes. Larger changes: at or above the training median. Retention is one-sided (a grasp that shrank still scores near one), and the cosine ignores overall scale. A stricter exact-direction measure gives lower medians: 0.66 (TACO), 0.75 (ARCTIC).

Interpretation

Dense contact trajectories mix structural change with realization variation that, in the typical event, leaves the modelled wrench capability nearly unchanged.

Can the structural part be isolated?

Observation 3

Can the functionally meaningful part of dense contact be represented more compactly?

A compact description keeps much of what matters: which hand parts touch, how much, roughly where, facing which way.

TACO ARCTIC

Each frame is described at eight levels; fixed small models recover its modelled wrench profile (itself computed from contact positions and directions) and predict the compact quantities and that profile 1, 4 and 8 frames ahead. A ranking of inputs, not absolute prediction.

TACO ARCTIC Source: description-ladder analysis

Wrench-profile error (relative L1): 0.35 → 0.18 (TACO), 0.51 → 0.18 (ARCTIC) with coarse position and direction.

The remaining dense detail is real

From the compact state alone, a small fitted model leaves 11% (TACO) and 26% (ARCTIC) of the contact-map variance unexplained.

That remainder persists from frame to frame; the predictors we tested capture little of its change.

TACO ARCTIC
Technical note

The compact level is called R2 (48 numbers per frame). These are comparisons between inputs under fixed small models, on the levels we tested (nothing between 12 and 48 numbers was tried); they are not statements of absolute predictability or information content, and the compact level is close to, not at, the lowest error. Dense inputs score worse under these fixed models; that is no statement about the information they hold. The dense inputs remain better at predicting the dense future itself. The residual is what one particular reconstruction model leaves. TACO and ARCTIC only.

Interpretation

Dense contact reads as a structured contact state plus realization detail.

How should such a structured state evolve over time?

Current research direction

Toward structured contact dynamics

Can human contact dynamics be modelled as the evolution of a compact structural state, while dense contact detail is treated as realization variation?

We are currently investigating how such structural states should evolve over time and how realization detail should be preserved.

This is a direction: no method and no result is claimed.
